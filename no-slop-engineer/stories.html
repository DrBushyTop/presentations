Story options · 30 September 2026

Three other ways to tell it.

All three keep the same argument, the same 52 minutes of material, 14 minutes of demos and most of the sources. They change the order and the thing that carries the story.

Timings are estimates, not rehearsed. Each running order includes the 30-second Jev aside. Q&A and demo slack sit outside the 52 minutes, as in the current plan.

Current plan

One change, all the way through

  • It works as a teaching sequence. People can follow it, and every demo has a reason to be there.
  • The order is the research, plan, implement lifecycle with a case study on top. Experienced engineers will guess the next section by minute ten.
  • The leak, which is the payoff, shows up around minute 24. Until then the audience is watching someone be careful, with no failure yet to show why it matters.
  • One seeded bug has to carry 52 minutes. If someone in the room spots it at minute 3, the tension is gone.
  • The counterargument lands at minute 43, when people are already tired. It gets less weight than it deserves.

Replay forward

Run the same change forward in 90 seconds, this time with every artifact in place. Then ask the room: which of these stages is missing in your repo?

Why it could work

  • The failure comes first, so the suspense is about where it should have been stopped. Knowing the bug doesn't spoil anything.
  • Senior engineers read postmortems all the time. Each practice arrives as the answer to a concrete miss, not as advice.
  • Production and the counterargument move from minute 43 to minute 4.

What could go wrong

  • Reverse order is hard to follow without a constant signal. It needs a rewind bar on every slide.
  • It can drift into blaming the agent. Keep it blameless: humans approved it too.
  • Avoid the "cost of a defect rises over time" curve. Everyone has seen it.

00–03

The rules

Define slop and explain how voting works. Hands are enough. A poll app adds setup risk for little gain.

03–09

Round 1: 1,900 lines or 10?

A generated rename across the codebase next to a 10-line authorization change. Which one deserves the review time? The right call is to ship the rename with a mechanical check and block the 10 lines. That leads into review units, Dillon, stacks and Spotify.

09–15

Round 2: "The table view already filters by team"

The agent's research summary makes a confident claim. Trace it back to the code live. It's true for the view, but not for the new path.

15–22

Round 3: A beautiful plan

The plan is long, tidy and approved, but it never says what happens when the team parameter is forged. Cover Honeycomb's plan reviews and Chopin or Spec Kit in one minute.

22–33

Round 4: The export

Green tests and a clean diff. Most of the room will ship it. Then the verification demo: the canary row, the boundary check and the negative control.

33–40

Round 5: "LGTM" after three fix attempts

The last green run belongs to the previous commit. This round covers bounded loops and the stop path.

40–48

Round 6: Would you let a machine approve this?

A narrow change with its telemetry attached, auto-approved the way Intercom does it. The honest answer is probably yes. Bring in Polylane's production review, Uber and the Jev aside, and give the counterargument its best round.

48–52

The scoreboard

How many rounds did the room get right, and what changed their votes? Ask everyone to pick the round their team would have lost and fix that one on Monday.

Why it could work

  • It has the most energy. Engineers enjoy judging code, and the room is committed from minute one.
  • Each practice arrives right after a mistake the audience made themselves.
  • The rounds stand alone, so a 45-minute version just drops a round.

What could go wrong

  • If the answer is always "block", the room works it out by round 3. At least two rounds (1 and 6) must be "ship".
  • It can feel like a quiz show and lose depth. The export round needs its full 11 minutes.
  • Six fake PRs have to look real and still be readable at slide size. That's the main cost.

My take

Start from option 1, and borrow from 2 and 3

Your doubt about the current spine is justified. The problem isn't that it follows one change. It shows the failure too late and marches through the lifecycle in order. Option 1 fixes both for about half a day of extra work, and keeps the export case you've already scoped.

  1. Borrow one vote from option 2. Before the reveal in the opening, ask "would you have merged this?" The room commits, and the incident lands harder.
  2. Tell the loop section in the first person, from option 3. The review bot and Ralph loops come from your own posts. Two minutes of "here's what I built and where it stopped" is worth more there than a diagram.
  3. Pick option 2 or 3 only if you want a different talk. Option 2 has the most energy and the most prep. Option 3 depends on episodes only you know about. If you remember a real near-miss that's better than the export fixture, option 3 is the strongest of the lot.

Also considered

Left out

The delegation dial. One slider from "human writes everything" to "software factory", and the talk moves it one notch at a time. It's too close to the "Who still needs to understand?" route that's already in the workbench.

Build the reviewer live. Grow a review bot on stage and find what it can't judge. The source notes already warn against a tour of the bot, and a live build is the riskiest demo on the list.
